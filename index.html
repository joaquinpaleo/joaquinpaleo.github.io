Job Market Paper

Grades, Academic Performance, and Labor Market Outcomes

with Mathías Fondo and Rodrigo Nicolau

Draft dated August 2026.

Abstract

Grades open doors to scholarships, graduate programs, and jobs, but distinguishing the effect of grades from the underlying ability they measure is hard. We study this question by implementing a Regression Discontinuity Design around grade-jump cutoffs at Uruguay’s main higher education institution, where a deterministic rule converts continuous course scores into coarser transcript grades. Marginally earning a higher first-semester grade raises next-semester GPA by 0.23 standard deviations. Because students observe both the score and the grade, the response reflects the transcript label rather than anything students learn about their own ability. In the labor market, monthly earnings at the first job show no detectable change. In contrast, hourly wages are 17 to 21 percent higher, while weekly hours worked are 2.2 to 2.8 hours lower. The wage premium fades quickly, but the hours reduction persists. These findings seem to be driven by within-sector changes, rather than students sorting across sectors with systematically different jobs. A higher grade also raises public-sector entry by 7 percentage points, where hiring rules score GPA explicitly. Finally, among students who start working after the grade is realized, we find evidence that academic performance improves in the semesters after the first job begins, relative to students who marginally earned a lower grade, which is consistent with them working fewer hours.

Working Papers

Close-Election Unionization and the Demand for College

Draft, June 2026.

Abstract

A large literature shows that positive shocks to local blue-collar wages discourage young workers from attending college by raising the opportunity cost of schooling. I ask whether unionization, a central but understudied source of variation in the non-college wage, operates through the same channel. I assemble a panel of 270,042 NLRB representation elections (1959–2024) and exploit close elections, in which a union barely wins versus barely loses, as locally exogenous variation in the intensity of new unionization within a county. Aggregating close-election outcomes to the county level following the regression-discontinuity-aggregation (RDA) framework, I estimate the effect of new unionization on two complementary outcomes: the residence-based stock of college-educated adults (decennial Census and ACS tabulations via NHGIS, 1980–2020) and institution-based community-college enrollment (IPEDS, 1986–2022). I find that a one-standard-deviation increase in close-election unionization reduces the local bachelor’s-degree share among men by roughly 3.9 percent (β = −0.024, p ≈ 0.007 on the full five-decade panel); the effect is six times larger and concentrated in the youngest (18–24) cohort whose enrollment decisions overlap the treatment window, and is accompanied by no change in adult population, ruling out spatial sorting. An event-study design on community-college enrollment shows a sharp, placebo-validated decline four to eight years after close-election union activity (β = −0.21, p ≈ 0.003), specific to the public two-year sector. County-average payroll does not move at any horizon, ruling out a correlated labor-demand boom and locating the mechanism in the expected value of rationed union jobs to marginal enrollees rather than in the average local wage. The estimates are robust to bandwidth, weighting, and fixed-effects choices, and their magnitude is comparable to, though somewhat larger than, the schooling responses documented for housing, coal, and fracking booms. The results identify organized labor as a quantitatively meaningful determinant of local human-capital accumulation and provide the first close-election causal evidence on the union–schooling margin.

Work in Progress

Passing a Course as Treatment

In many first-semester courses a minimum score on the midterms decides whether a student is exempted from the final exam. I use these within-course thresholds in a regression discontinuity design to ask whether clearing an institutional bar changes a student’s progression through the degree, as distinct from the measured-performance margin the job market paper studies.

Relative Grades and Academic Achievement

Course rosters at the university are ordered alphabetically, so two students with the same grade in the same course see different information about their standing relative to the classmates listed next to them. I use this variation to study whether performing well relative to one’s immediate peers affects subsequent academic outcomes, over and above the grade itself.
